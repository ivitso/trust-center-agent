01 · Market
The market was about to catch up.

Competitors were starting to talk about AI, but none had shipped an agentic Trust Center experience yet.

▾
02 · Customers
Every security team was excited AND afraid.

The same admins who wanted an agent also feared the complications of security: Product lines, access groups, secret documents. An agent needed to respond based on the user's permissions.

▾
03 · Risk
The riskiest tool call was the most valuable.

The questionnaire answering tool call would allow users to drop a 1000 question xlsx and get back a cited CSV with answers, sources, and next steps. This had the highest value, but also most feared.

▾
Why this brief was harder than "add AI"

A security prospect visits a Trust Center, downloads a SOC 2, checks the compliance badges, and leaves. Except they almost never actually leave with everything they came for. Most return with follow-up questions sent to their customer service, sales, or security team partner. As those teams get smaller in the age of AI, the volume of inbound only grows. An unanswered question can stall a deal, and a slow response can lose one entirely.

This bet was pushing toward a future where visitors were unafraid to interact with a Trust Center agent. One that knew the documents, respected the access gates, and escalated what it couldn't safely answer. The goal was 95% touchless reviews by the end of 2026, meaning users can find what they need in the Trust Center 95% of the time, without ever reaching out for extra help to partner teams.

This first meant we needed to ship an MVP within two months, with a squad that was still onboarding to the product. We were learning the domain, building the feature, and defining the category all at the same time.

The fears we heard from admins were not abstract. They could picture hallucinations surfacing wrong answers. They could picture gated content leaking across product lines. They could picture accidental disclosure of documents meant for a different access group. Every admin we spoke to could name a specific scenario that scared them.

03 · Orient

How three tool calls all run through one permission layer.

Before we touched the chat surface, the design work started with understanding what happened underneath. Every visitor query had to route to the right tool call, and every response had to pass through the same permission layer. The chat UI was the wrapper. The real complexity was in the routing.

User flow · Sept 2025
The routing logic we mapped before touching the chat surface
We mapped every path a visitor could take: NDA empty states, product line confirmation, feedback loops per tool call, fallback states for vague prompts, and the escalation path that routes unanswerable questions to a Slack notification. This flow became the source of truth for engineering.
Constraints we designed around
  • Two months to MVP for Bundle and Ask tool calls. One additional month for the Questionnaire tool call.
  • Access group complexity: every customer configured document permissions differently, with different levels of gating per visitor.
  • Product lines: larger companies had sub-brands that each needed their own scope. Intuit, for example, had TurboTax, Credit Karma, Mailchimp, ProConnect, and QuickBooks as five separate product lines, each requiring both permission access and correct product line routing.
  • One week to build all three flows in Lovable, get design partner feedback, then move into final UI in Figma.

04 · Prototyping in Lovable

How I prototyped 3 major agent flows in a week.

"Prototyping agentic flows in Lovable was new for me and new for Conveyor. Interactions were still very linear, but it was important to map out the main success and failure flows for the team to decide on before finessing the base and system prompts."

I partnered with my PM to review designs and general visuals, and then with my entire squad for the UX interactions to make sure all of us were on the same page with these agentic flows. Although linearly prototyped, we were able to get a second round of customer feedback through these prototypes and better understand what questions users might have, what they thought might not work, and any concerns or excitements they could surface. We got great feedback: What about changing product lines in the middle of an interaction? How do we make sure admins can see agent analytics? What if a user only wanted one of the three tool calls, could each be individually toggled? How would a user access a download-only document that is not viewable? How does someone interact with a locked document that requires a special password? These were all questions that sharpened our edge cases and gave us a much stronger foundation for the next round of design.

What was hard about prototyping agentic flows in 2025

Lovable was newer at Conveyor. Most product surfaces were designed in Figma and built straight to staging. For the agent, neither was an option. Figma couldn't simulate the conversational rhythm. Staging wasn't ready. Lovable let me build clickable prototypes with scripted AI responses, which let us test interaction model and tone before committing to a full Figma spec.

The hard part was that conversational flows are not linear. Each tool call had at least three failure modes (no access, no answer, partial answer) and a feedback loop. Capturing those in a prototype meant building eight to ten distinct end-states per flow, not three.

07 · Findings

What we found, and the one sequencing bet that followed.

Three findings reframe agentic UX. The fourth is about release order, not feature scope.

01 · The Primitive Finding

Tool calls, not screens, are the unit of agentic design.

The chat surface looked the same across all three flows. The work was inside the surface. Bundle needed retrieval plus a download manifest. Ask needed retrieval plus citation rendering plus click-through-to-source. Answer needed a loop over the other two plus a policy engine. We stopped designing screens and started designing the tool call's full path: input, retrieval, generation, citation, escalation, output.

"I want the documents and get in and out. Get the docs and pen test, get out as quick as I can."M.M. · Greenhouse (testing Bundle)
"The direct quote from the documentation - proof is in the pudding."K.J. · Klaviyo (testing Ask)
02 · The Permission Finding

Citations are the permission to act.

Every cited response had to show source. Without a visible source, even a correct answer was unusable to a security reviewer. We designed cited-chip responses with snippet preview, click-through to the source document, and scroll-to-position highlight. The citation was not a footer. The citation was the answer. The prose was the wrapper.

"Citations and source transparency are non-negotiable."M.M. · Greenhouse
"Our legal team is hesitant on what is generated. There's concern that we'll have a generated answer sent out and we won't have eyes on it."L.M. · Quantum Metric
03 · The Escalation Finding

Escalation is the primary UI of the riskiest tool call.

Answer a Questionnaire shipped because the agent knew when not to answer. Words like "breach," "incident," "outage," and "active investigation" had to route to a human queue, never auto-answer. We designed three escalation affordances: a per-question flag visible to the visitor, a batched admin queue with context, and a downloadable summary that distinguished AI-answered from human-pending.

"Having those topics escalated to us would be huge to enable us to have more trust in the system providing customers answers."N.E. · Workday
"Initially both the legal and security team are going to be very nervous about allowing people to upload questionnaires."L.M. · Quantum Metric
04 · The Sequencing Finding

Staged delivery converts fear into opt-in.

The bet was not which features to ship. The bet was which order to ship them in. Labs (November) put Bundle and Ask in front of conservative customers with no Answer in sight. MVP at GA (December) hardened the same two tool calls and added admin reporting. Answer a Questionnaire at GA (January) earned its way in. By January, the customers who had told us "not ready" in September were the first wave of design partners on the riskiest flow.

Competitive research · Sept 2025
No competitor had shipped an agentic experience
Trust Center vendors were starting to talk about AI but the live offerings were still static portals with search bars. The three-tool-call architecture and the permission layer underneath became the differentiator that bought us category-creation room.
How we placed against competitors

The competitive scan ran across the Trust Center vendor landscape and a handful of adjacent security tools. None had shipped a true agentic experience yet. A few had announced AI roadmaps. The window we had was real but narrow.

Conveyor's existing Trust Center already had structured documents, permission groups, and NDA handling - which meant the agent could be built on top of infrastructure that already respected the gates. That accelerated the build versus a greenfield agentic competitor.

09 · Launch & Enablement

Why letting admins turn the agent off got them to try it on.

"We made a deliberate choice to give admins a toggle. Enable the agent at the Trust Center level, enable questionnaires as a separate flag, disable the whole thing immediately if they got cold feet. The opt-out is what got the opt-in."

Shipping the agent meant more than shipping the code. Marketing, sales enablement, white-glove customer onboarding, and a deliberate accommodation for AI-skeptical admins all had to ship alongside.

Featured design decision

AI-skeptical accommodation

  • Admin toggle to enable the agent per Trust Center
  • Questionnaire flag as a separate, independent setting
  • Hard disable at any time, no migration penalty
  • Result: conservative customers who said "not ready" in September enabled the agent in December

11 · What Came Next

Why we shipped Looker first, and native analytics next.

The Looker dashboard we shipped at launch was a beta. It taught us what admins actually wanted to see. The native in-app analytics build is in design now, with that learning baked in.

Hero follow-on

Looker reports were the prototype. Native analytics is the product.

The Looker pipeline let us learn fast: which questions weren't answered, where users hit dead ends, which trust pillars admins cared about most. Now we're designing the in-product version: an admin dashboard that surfaces unanswered questions, friction points, agent performance over time, and the questions visitors are asking that the knowledge base can't yet support. The scrappy version proved the appetite. The native version is the product.

12 · Reflection

What I'm taking forward.

Seven lessons that travel to the next agentic product.

01

Speed is a design feature.

Two months to MVP forced cleaner decisions. Anything that couldn't justify its weight against the timeline got cut. The constraint sharpened the spec.

02

Tool calls, not screens, are the unit of agentic design.

The chat surface is the wrapper. The work is in the tool call's full path: input, retrieval, generation, citation, escalation, output.

03

Citations are the permission to act.

A correct answer without a visible source is unusable to a security reviewer. The citation is the answer. The prose is the wrapper.

04

Escalation is the primary UI of the riskiest tool call.

The agent ships because it knows when not to answer. Design the policy surface, the admin queue, and the visitor handoff before you design the happy path.

05

Permission-aware AI is invisible work.

Access groups, NDA gates, doc-level perms, and session tokens are the agent's first impression. The customer never sees them. That invisibility is the product.

06

The opt-out is design.

Letting AI-skeptical admins turn the agent off is what got them to turn it on. The toggle is not a feature concession. It's the affordance that built trust.

07

Continuous research beats kick-off research.

Research between releases is more valuable than research before them. Every ship is a re-interview opportunity. Card sort in September, Lovable testing in October, Labs interviews in November, GA feedback in December, post-launch retro in January. Each round changed the next release.
