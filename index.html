01 · The Brief

What is a Trust Center - and why ship first.

A Trust Center is where companies publish their security posture - SOC 2s, policies, NDAs - so prospects can evaluate them mid-deal. Unanswered questions stall deals and pile onto shrinking support teams, so we bet on an agent to reach 95% touchless reviews. Research came first: what visitors needed, what admins wanted to deflect, and which AI behaviors felt safe versus reckless.

What our design partners taught us
Access & NDAs

Reviewers wanted certainty about who could see what.

NDAs run through DocuSign, and role-based access governs every document. → We required sign-in and a signed NDA to use the agent - no anonymous visitors, for security and correct permissions.

Download behavior

Reviewers hated clicking through docs one at a time.

Docs are previewed and downloaded with watermarks, but they'd grab everything and sort it later. → So the agent bundles the relevant set into a one-click "Download All" zip.

Questionnaires

Even a 50%-complete questionnaire is a win.

If the answers are sourced, it gets them started on the work. → We optimized for a cited, partial draft they can finish - not a brittle end-to-end auto-fill.

Disclaimer language

A tiny note became a blocker to adoption.

For major customers the AI disclaimer wasn't a footnote - their legal teams wanted to write and approve it. → We made the disclaimer text configurable per trust center.

Why this brief was harder than "add AI"

A security prospect visits a Trust Center, downloads a SOC 2, checks the compliance badges, and leaves. Except they almost never actually leave with everything they came for. Most return with follow-up questions sent to their customer service, sales, or security team partner. As those teams get smaller in the age of AI, the volume of inbound only grows. An unanswered question can stall a deal, and a slow response can lose one entirely.

This bet was pushing toward a future where visitors were unafraid to interact with a Trust Center agent. One that knew the documents, respected the access gates, and escalated what it couldn't safely answer. The goal was 95% touchless reviews by the end of 2026, meaning users can find what they need in the Trust Center 95% of the time, without ever reaching out for extra help to partner teams.

This first meant we needed to ship an MVP within two months, with a squad that was still onboarding to the product. We were learning the domain, building the feature, and defining the category all at the same time.

The fears we heard from admins were not abstract. They could picture hallucinations surfacing wrong answers. They could picture gated content leaking across product lines. They could picture accidental disclosure of documents meant for a different access group. Every admin we spoke to could name a specific scenario that scared them.

03 · Orient

How three tool calls all run through one permission layer.

Before touching the chat surface, I mapped the interactions - who asks (visitors and our own sales team), who answers, who sets the rules - then the routing underneath. Every query had to reach the right tool call, and every response pass through the same permission layer.

The hard part wasn't answering, it was not answering. Emailing the admin was the obvious fallback, but a self-serve visitor in their inbox meant they'd fallen below the white-glove line. So an unanswered question doesn't route a person: it's logged as a knowledge gap. The visitor gets an honest “not documented yet,” the corpus gets stronger, and the admin sees it in analytics instead of their inbox. Goal: trust the agent to represent the company accurately.
Fear: it says something wrong, or shows a document to the wrong visitor. Goal: get the docs and answers they came for, fast.
Fear: a dead end, or chasing emails to finish a security review. Two ways to the same answer. A visitor can self-serve directly - or get white-glove help from the vendor's own sales team, who use the very same agent to pull answers and documents for a prospect mid-deal. One agent and one permission layer served both the outside visitor and the inside seller - which is why a healthy share of usage was internal, alongside the 69% that was visitor-driven.
User flow · Sept 2025
The routing logic we mapped before touching the chat surface
Every path a visitor could take: NDA empty states, product-line confirmation, per-tool feedback loops, fallbacks for vague prompts, and the escalation that routes unanswerable questions to Slack. This became engineering's source of truth.
Constraints we designed around
  • Two months to a working Bundle and Ask beta. One additional month for the Questionnaire tool call.
  • Access group complexity: every customer configured document permissions differently, with different levels of gating per visitor.
  • Product lines: larger companies had sub-brands that each needed their own scope - Intuit alone had five (TurboTax, Credit Karma, Mailchimp, ProConnect, QuickBooks), each needing its own permissions and routing.
  • One week to build all three flows in Lovable, get design partner feedback, then move into final UI in Figma.

What was hard about prototyping agentic flows in 2025

Lovable was newer at Conveyor. Most product surfaces were designed in Figma and built straight to staging. For the agent, neither was an option. Figma couldn't simulate the conversational rhythm. Staging wasn't ready. Lovable let me build clickable prototypes with scripted AI responses, which let us test interaction model and tone before committing to a full Figma spec.

The hard part was that conversational flows are not linear. Each tool call had at least three failure modes (no access, no answer, partial answer) and a feedback loop. Capturing those in a prototype meant building eight to ten distinct end-states per flow, not three.

08 · Ship

The design considerations underneath our Agent.

With clients ranging from excited to scared, we needed the agent to feel effortless on day one, with deliberate accommodations for AI-skeptics and an experience far beyond the automated bots people dread.

Tool call · Bundle

Bundle Documents

Every customer we spoke to had the same first instinct: find the documents they need and download them. The frustration was always in the search, not the download.

Admins already know the standard bundle that covers most requests, so the agent surfaces a Featured Documents section automatically as a one-click zip. One click in place of ten.

Tool call · Ask

Ask a Question

AI-skeptics did not want to trust a generated response, and both admins and visitors worried about hallucinations and who would be held responsible for one.

We decided security professionals needed exact quotes and the source documents they came from. On click, users can open the document and see highlighted content where the response originated.

Tool call · Answer

Answer a Questionnaire

The biggest time sink for Trust Center visitors is manually filling out security questionnaires. If a user could upload one and get it returned complete, that alone would be transformative.

But admins needed proof it worked before they would trial it. That led us to build analytics and insights so admins could see exactly what the agent was providing to their customers.

Designing for AI uncertainty

The real question was never "build a chatbot" - it was how a security reviewer trusts an AI answer when a wrong one means a failed audit. A few deliberate mechanisms did that, plus the decisions and launch work around them. Tab through each.

Confidence, scored - not guessed. Every answer gets a rating - Green (exact match to an approved answer), Blue (high confidence, strong sources), or Yellow (uncertain). Admins set the release policy on each tier, so the agent auto-sends what it's sure of and holds the rest for a human.

The ultimate override is a kill switch - admins can turn the agent off in one click, per Trust Center or per questionnaire. Betting on that opt-out is what drove opt-in: customers who said "not ready" had it toggled on by December.

The admin sets the bar. Drag to choose how confident the agent must be before it answers on its own. Below the line it holds for a human first - and it only ever answers with a citation.

One dial lets a nervous admin ship cautiously and a confident one move fast, with no code change.

LowMediumHighExact match

Continuous evals graded every answer against a gold-standard set - a ~0.1% hallucination rate on questionnaires - and the team spot-checked 10% of conversations in LangSmith, scoring each on five dimensions: clarity, completeness, correctness, relevance, and safety.

Flagged responses fed straight back into the system prompt and retrieval tuning between releases - surfacing hallucinations and permission edge cases early, and driving latency from ~30s at beta to 5.7s by swapping LLM-based document selection for deterministic search plus a model upgrade.

Design the tool call, not the screen. The chat surface looked the same for all three flows. The real design work was the path underneath: input, retrieval, generation, citation, escalation, output.

Citations are the permission to act. Without a visible source, even a correct answer was unusable to a security reviewer.

Escalation is the primary UI. The agent shipped because it knew when not to answer.

I owned launch readiness end-to-end - marketing (product-page and social assets, launch video, blog), sales enablement (deck, objection-handling guide, FAQ), and updated agent docs, all shipped alongside the product.

Then I stayed close to adoption: white-glove setup for every early customer, weekly reviews of usage and response quality, and manual Looker reporting until in-app analytics shipped. 3+ early adopters live in December.

What usage behavior told us

  • Customers have a clear goal: completing a security review. The agent is trusted as a source of truth during that process.
  • Interactions are focused on specific artifacts, not exploration. Engagement depth suggests real usage, not casual browsing.
  • I flagged that a legacy feature - the "Questionnaire Eliminator," a slow single-question modal - would "eat" the new agent and confuse users. At launch, some admins tried the old feature for the first time; I advocated for users and built the deprecation plan to migrate them onto the agent before GA.

What we learned

  • There is no clear signal today that a review is "complete" or that the user has gathered all the details they need.
  • General queries via LangSmith show visitors using the agent to find SOC 2 Type II reports, data security and handling info, and cyber insurance details. Conversations show users moving through related questions.
  • User adoption is highly varied: some customers like Carta, Lucid, and Zendesk have had rapid visitor adoption, while others remain minimal or only have internal users accessing the agent.

How this guides future direction

  • Expanding agent knowledge beyond Documents and Knowledge Base was top priority: adding subprocessors, announcements, and other page sources, along with possible external sources like main pages or status pages.
  • Specific verbiage for AI disclaimers was much more important than expected for individual customers, even though we used industry-standard language. This was an urgent but small ask to unblock major customers.
  • Allowing customers to receive analytics reports and view messages asked of the agent excited admins, giving us a clear "go" to continue improving analytics from a Looker report to our internal Conveyor dashboards.

10 · Figma Flows

Final pixel-perfect flows and passoff in Figma.

Shipping five parallel workstreams took constant advocacy - for users against a confusing legacy feature, for engineers to invest in reusable components, and for data-sized scoping when edge cases spooked the team. Clearly mapped Figma flows kept everyone aligned as new members ramped up.

Onboarding a growing team

Several designers and engineers joined mid-project. I onboarded the new designers and mapped the flows into clear workstreams, so five parallel tracks shipped without gaps.

Aligning on edge cases with data

As we spoke with users, edge cases surfaced that sent the team into a panic. I'd pause and ask for the metrics - how often does this really happen, which accounts hit it, how many times in the past year - so engineering could size the actual risk and safely defer the rare ones instead of building for every hypothetical.

Advocating for a component system

Conveyor had inherited only a baseline buttons-and-text file, and as new designers joined and AI spread through the product, the drift showed. I advocated for engineering to build components into Storybook for reuse - starting with the new chat patterns I saw could be reused across the whole app.

11 · Reflection

What I'm taking forward.

Learning and tradeoffs to take to the next agentic product.

01

Data turns skeptics into champions.

Scrappy Looker reports and emailed weekly summaries gave admins visibility into what their visitors were actually asking. Once they could see the agent working, adoption stopped being a sell and started being a pull.

02

Agentic design is more than the seed prompts.

Even though the chat surface is a wrapper around the agent prompting, it's important to help build trust for the user. The language of the agent, tone of voice, and how it responded in error states were carefully considered UX points and iterated on. With no established design system and new team members joining mid-project, every component had to be built for reuse and every flow mapped for handoff.

03

Continuous feedback is better than one kick-off round.

We were able to do initial discussions, Lovable prototype feedback, and then Beta release to our own Trust Center, allowing users to get a feel of what the interactions were before releasing to GA. We also gave white glove support for major customers to ensure their onboarding was successful.

04

Permissions work is invisible work.

Access groups, NDA gating, doc-level permissions, session tokens, and translations are all part of the first impression of the agent. However, until they are missing, the customer never realizes they're there.

05

The opt-out is design.

Letting AI-skeptical admins toggle the agent on or off at any time - and test as their own user groups - is what got them to turn it on. I defaulted every customer to agent ON with a one-click off; it drew some opt-out-vs-opt-in complaints and support tickets, but adoption hit 61% and very few actually turned it off. The opt-out affordance was the thing that built trust.

06

Name the confusion before it compounds.

The overlap between the old Questionnaire Eliminator and the new agent caused friction with both customers and internal teams. Identifying that confusion early and creating a deprecation plan prevented it from undermining the launch.

07

More proof isn't more trust.

V1 showed the full quoted source alongside every answer. After users pushed back, I cut it for clickable citations. The cost was less immediate proof the AI wasn't hallucinating - you had to click to verify - but people actually read and trusted the answers. More information had reduced trust, not increased it.

08

Earn trust before the biggest ask.

The questionnaire was the #1 request but also the highest-risk tool call, so I deferred it ~14 weeks to January. Our most enthusiastic customers had to wait for the thing they wanted most - but by the time it shipped, the first two tool calls had earned enough trust that questionnaire adoption was smooth.
